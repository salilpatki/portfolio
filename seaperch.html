Overview

For the 2026 SeaPerch competition, our team ("Subatomic Submarines") designed a torpedo-inspired, three-section modular ROV optimized for speed, stability, and precision. Using the Engineering Design Process, we iteratively designed, tested, and refined each subsystem based on measured data — with two of our final design choices actually contradicting our initial hypotheses. The complete vehicle cost just $124.78.

The Challenge

The Obstacle Course demanded rapid navigation through five angled 18" hoops, while the Mission Course required precise manipulation to complete four tasks worth 110 points — repairing a bridge, surveying a dam, clearing debris, and sampling water. Our design had to balance speed, maneuverability, and stability at once.

Modular Torpedo Architecture

We chose a three-section modular body sealed with O-rings, letting us develop and test each subsystem independently and make fast field repairs. The front houses the camera and nose cone, the center holds the Venturi propulsion junction, and the rear contains the electronics. The torpedo form minimizes hydrodynamic drag while keeping the build compact and robust.

Hydrodynamic Nose Cone — CFD Study

Using OpenFOAM CFD simulations, I compared four nose geometries under identical conditions: flat (Cd ≈ 1.10), cone (Cd ≈ 0.50), ogive (Cd ≈ 0.42), and hemisphere (Cd ≈ 0.35). We hypothesized the sharper ogive or cone would win — but the hemisphere achieved the lowest drag, ~17% below the ogive and ~68% below the flat design.

At the low Reynolds numbers of our operating regime, maintaining attached flow (Coandă effect) matters more than a sharp frontal profile. We paired the winning shape with a clear resin hemispherical dome housing a forward-facing fishing camera — the curved dome eliminates water refraction distortion, giving the pilot accurate depth perception for precise object alignment.

Venturi Tube Propulsion

Instead of exposed propellers, we developed an integrated Venturi propulsion system that accelerates water through a converging inlet, narrow throat (housing the motor/propeller), and expanding diffuser. Two horizontal tubes provide forward/reverse thrust; one vertical tube handles depth control.

The design applies the continuity equation (A₁v₁ = A₂v₂) and Bernoulli's principle. With a 2.0" inlet narrowing to a 1.0" throat, inlet flow at 1.8 m/s theoretically accelerates to ~7.2 m/s, predicting ~19.7 N of thrust. Real-world friction, turbulence, and propeller efficiency bring measured peak thrust to 4.5 N at 9820 RPM — the gap itself was a valuable lesson in theoretical vs. actual performance.

I force-tested four Venturi geometries in water against a bare-motor control; the optimized 4.88" design (Tube D) won, ~4.7% above the next best. The small margin revealed diminishing returns.

Electronics & Control

The ROV runs on an Arduino Uno with an Ethernet Shield housed in a custom waterproof enclosure. Two L298N dual motor drivers control the three thrusters, converting the Arduino's digital and PWM signals into the correct voltage and direction.

  • Two analog joysticks independently drive the horizontal thrusters; two push buttons control the vertical thruster
  • PWM on the L298N enable pins gives smooth speed control
  • ±10 joystick dead zones prevent jitter when sticks are centered
  • 12V power delivered via XT30 connectors for safe, high-current connections

Mission Hook — One Tool, All Four Tasks

Rather than swapping tools, we designed a single double-edge hook: the top edge hooks and lifts, the bottom edge pulls and drags. This one tool completes all four mission tasks — retrieving floats, sliding the repair pipe, inserting the dam plug, rotating the flood gate, and transporting debris — with no changes. The entire body is 3D printed in PLA (594.56 g total) for a strong, lightweight structure.

What I Learned

The strongest lesson was data-driven decision-making — both final choices (Venturi Tube D and the hemispherical nose) contradicted our hypotheses, and trusting the data produced a better vehicle. Comparing my ~19.7 N theoretical thrust to the 4.5 N measured value also gave me real intuition for how friction, turbulence, and propeller efficiency erode ideal performance. Next steps: smoothing internal Venturi surfaces to cut turbulence and adding sensor feedback for partial automation.
