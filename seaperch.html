SeaPerch Underwater ROV

CAD · CFD (OpenFOAM) · Venturi Propulsion · 3D Printing · Systems Design

🏆 4th of 9,000+ teams worldwide · Innovation Award

Overview

For the 2026 SeaPerch competition, our team ("Subatomic Submarines") designed a torpedo-inspired, submarine-style ROV optimized for speed, stability, and precision across the Mission and Obstacle Courses. I contributed to the mechanical design and hydrodynamic optimization using the Engineering Design Process — iteratively designing, testing, and refining each subsystem based on measured data. The final build cost just $124.78.

The Challenge

The Obstacle Course demanded rapid navigation through five angled 18" hoops, while the Mission Course required precise manipulation of objects — repairing a bridge, surveying a dam, clearing debris, and sampling water. This meant balancing three often-conflicting goals: speed, maneuverability, and stability, all within strict size and material constraints.

Modular Torpedo Architecture

We chose a three-section modular body sealed with O-rings and bolts, letting us develop and test each subsystem independently and disassemble quickly during limited pool time. The front houses the camera and nose cone, the center holds the Venturi propulsion system, and the rear contains the electronics, linear actuator, and stabilization components.

Hydrodynamic Nose Cone — CFD Study

Using OpenFOAM CFD simulations, I compared four nose geometries under identical conditions: flat (Cd ≈ 1.10), cone (Cd ≈ 0.50), ogive (Cd ≈ 0.42), and hemisphere (Cd ≈ 0.35). We hypothesized the sharper ogive or cone would win — but the hemisphere achieved the lowest drag, ~17% below the ogive and ~68% below the flat design.

At the low Reynolds numbers of our operating regime, maintaining attached flow (Coandă effect) matters more than minimizing frontal sharpness. This let us keep flow attached and reduce separation. We paired it with a clear resin hemispherical nose housing an internal camera, which also reduced refraction and preserved the streamlined profile. A great lesson in trusting data over intuition.

Venturi Tube Propulsion

Instead of standard open propellers, we developed a Venturi-based propulsion system. Using the continuity equation and Bernoulli's principle, we reasoned that decreasing cross-sectional area accelerates water flow and increases thrust.

I tested four Venturi geometries against a bare-motor control by suspending each in water and measuring thrust with a force sensor. Tube D produced the highest average thrust at 4.5 N, ~4.7% above the next best. The small margin between the top designs revealed diminishing returns, so we finalized Tube D. Horizontal and vertical tubes provided propulsion and depth control respectively.

Stability & Control

A rear-mounted linear actuator dynamically shifts the center of mass, while stabilizing fins and distributed wheel weights minimize roll for steady, controlled motion. The onshore control system uses two custom PCBs, an Arduino, and an Xbox controller for secure, responsive piloting.

What I Learned

The biggest takeaway was data-driven decision-making — twice our test results (Venturi Tube D and the hemispherical nose) contradicted our hypotheses, and trusting the data produced a better vehicle. I also learned that optimizing subsystems in isolation isn't enough: small changes in weight, buoyancy, and propulsion alignment had large effects on overall stability, reinforcing a systems-level mindset. Next steps would be smoothing internal Venturi surfaces to cut turbulence and adding sensor feedback for partial automation.
